When Did Crime Change?

Using the combined 2003–2025 dataset, we track annual incident counts for eight focus crimes. Hover over the chart to inspect exact values; click legend entries to isolate individual categories.

Larceny/Theft dominance (2014–2018): Peaks around 2017, likely driven by high-volume property crime in tourist-heavy areas and Prop 47 (2014) reclassifying some thefts as misdemeanors, affecting reporting patterns.

COVID-19 impact (2020): Most categories drop sharply, lockdowns removed street-level opportunities for Robbery and Assault. Motor Vehicle Theft is the notable exception, rising as empty streets left cars unobserved.

The 2025 anomaly: A sudden spike across almost all categories, possibly a batch of backlogged data integrated into the API, or a genuine post-pandemic shift. The data alone cannot distinguish these explanations.
Assignment 1.2

Which Districts Are Different?

Rather than raw counts, we compute the over-representation ratio: P(crime | district) / P(crime). A value of 2 means that crime type is twice as common there as in the city overall.

The Tenderloin doesn't just have more drug offenses, it has a ratio that dwarfs every other district. That's not only a crime pattern. It's a policing pattern.
District focus: Tenderloin. A massive over-representation of Drug Offense incidents, ratio well above 1, reflects both genuine concentration (open-air drug markets, high density of social services) and enforcement concentration: heavier patrol presence generates more recorded incidents for the same underlying activity level than in residential districts like Richmond or Taraval.

How Is Crime Distributed Across the Day?

Violin plots show the full time-of-day distribution, not just the median, but the shape, spread, and density of when incidents cluster or thin out.

Drug Offense clusters mid-afternoon (12:00–16:00), consistent with daytime enforcement patterns in the Tenderloin.

Assault and Robbery extend into late-night hours, driven by nightlife activity on weekends.

Midnight wrap-around: Crimes peaking near midnight show a split at 0/23, numerically opposite but temporally adjacent. A circular axis would represent this more accurately.
Assignment 1.4

Is Crime Everywhere, or Just Somewhere?

We rank all locations by incident count and plot them on log-log axes. A straight line here is the signature of a power-law distribution, extreme concentration in a tiny fraction of locations.

Power law confirmed: The near-straight line on log-log axes shows that a tiny fraction of locations — roughly the top 5%, accounts for the majority of all recorded incidents.

Implication: The "average block" is a misleading statistic. Neighbourhood-level aggregation obscures extreme local concentration, making some areas appear safer, or more dangerous, than the block-level reality.
Assignment 1.5

Which Crimes Share a Weekly Rhythm?

For each crime we build a 168-value vector: incident counts for every hour of the week. We compare all pairs using closed-form linear regression and R². Hover over any panel for details.

Most correlated — Assault & Robbery: Both peak on weekend evenings, driven by the same nightlife dynamics. High R² reflects their shared social context.

Least correlated is Burglary & Robbery: Burglary tends toward daytime weekday patterns (targeting unoccupied homes and offices), while Robbery peaks at night and on weekends, almost opposite opportunity structures.
⚠️ Data caveat: All figures are based on reported incidents from a 100,000-row sample per API endpoint. Under-reporting, patrol concentration, and API data quality all influence the patterns. Treat as signals, not ground truth.
