Baltimore's Renaissance:
How a City Stopped Counting Its Dead

For most of the 21st century, Baltimore averaged over 300 homicides a year. Then something changed. This is what the streets say — and what the data confirms.

By: Benjamin Balslund (s253848)  ·  May 2026

← Back

I first became aware of this story back in 2018 — that was when I watched the Cannes-selected film Charm City. After that I started following the story evolving in the streets of Baltimore, mainly because my mom lived in the US at the time and often traveled in and around the city.

Skip to now. Around five months ago, Andrew Callaghan and the Channel 5 crew released Baltimore Streets — a short documentary exploring the "Renaissance" in Baltimore, a city long known for extreme violence. It examines how the city achieved a 54% reduction in homicides since 2020 by shifting away from traditional policing toward community-led intervention.

In this assignment I examine the correlation between community-led violence intervention and the historic decline in Baltimore's homicide rates, using the Channel 5 narrative to contextualize the human impact behind the statistics. The structure is simple: each section opens with a claim from the video, then dives into the data behind it.

The Turning Point

Mayor Brandon Scott opens the documentary with a single, striking claim: for the majority of his life, Baltimore had over 300 homicides every year. 2023 was when that streak broke. The data bears this out — but the story behind the numbers is more complicated than a simple drop.

↓ what the data shows

The drop is not subtle. From a peak of roughly 350 homicides in 2015–2017 — the years immediately following the Freddie Gray unrest — the city worked its way down through the 2020s to figures not seen since the early 2000s. Shootings, which had been climbing since 2013, show an equally dramatic reversal after 2019.

The Scott Doctrine: Fewer Arrests, Safer Streets

The instinct in American policing has historically been: more crime means more arrests. Baltimore tried that for decades. At 00:23:54, Mayor Scott delivers the counter-evidence in two numbers that are almost impossible to argue with: in 2004, Baltimore made 91,000 arrests. In 2023, they made 15,000 — and the city was safer.

↓ what the data shows
"It has never been about how many people we arrest. It's about making the city safer." — Mayor Brandon Scott  00:23:54

What replaced mass arrests is a network of formerly incarcerated credible messengers — the Safe Streets program — who operate 24/7 tip hotlines and intervene directly in escalating conflicts before they turn lethal. They don't carry weapons. They carry credibility.

Where the Violence Lives — and Where It's Being Interrupted

Safe Streets doesn't operate everywhere. It targets specific neighbourhoods — the ones where the risk is highest. At 00:24:55, the Mayor names two of them and points to something remarkable: these high-risk zones went an entire year without a single homicide.

↓ what the data shows

Brooklyn topping the list while also being a Safe Streets success story is not a contradiction — it's the point. The program doesn't pick easy wins. It goes where the problem is worst.

Not Gang War. Human Conflict.

Decades of crime reporting have framed urban violence as gang warfare — organized, territorial, and solvable through targeted prosecution. At 00:20:35, the documentary challenges that frame with a statistic that redefines the whole problem.

↓ what the data shows

If violence is driven by interpersonal conflict rather than organized crime, then the most effective intervention is someone who can get in the room before the gun comes out — not a police officer arriving afterward. This is what makes the Safe Streets model coherent as policy, not just as community goodwill.

What the Data Can and Cannot Tell Us

Crime datasets record reported incidents, not all crime that occurs. Areas with heavier police presence generate more reports for the same underlying rate of activity. Neighbourhoods with deep distrust of police may under-report significantly. The Open Baltimore dataset is no exception.

⚠️ Data caveat: These figures reflect reported incidents from the Open Baltimore Part 1 Crime Dataset (644,531 records, 2011–2024). Arrest data for pre-2020 years is drawn from official Baltimore PD annual reports as cited in the Channel 5 documentary. The causal link between Safe Streets programming and the homicide decline is consistent with the data's timing and geography, but cannot be proven by observational data alone. Demographic change, economic shifts, and COVID-19 disruption also play a role.

With those caveats in place: the pattern is consistent, geographically specific, and temporally coherent with the policy interventions described in the documentary. A 54% reduction in homicides in four years, concentrated in the neighbourhoods targeted by the program, is not a statistical artifact. Something real happened in Baltimore.

Andrew Callaghan went to find it. The data confirms it was there.

When 911 reroutes to mental health — the diversion pilot

Since June 2021, Baltimore has run a pilot programme where eligible 911 calls — mostly psychiatric crises, suicide alerts, and behavioural emergencies — are routed to the Baltimore Crisis Response Inc.
