Baltimore's Renaissance:
How a City Stopped Counting Its Dead

The last 10 years, Baltimore averaged over 300 homicides. Then something changed. This is what the streets say; and what the data confirms.

By Benjamin Balslund (s253848)  ·  May 2026

← Back

In late 2024, Andrew Callaghan and the Channel 5 crew released Baltimore Streets, a documentary exploring how a city long defined by its murder rate began, quietly, to turn things around. Since Mayor Brandon Scott took office in 2020, the claim is that homicides have fallen by 40%. This assignment examines what the data actually shows.

The structure is simple: each section opens with a moment from the video, then the data responds. Numbers come from the Open Baltimore Part 1 Crime Dataset, supplemented where necessary by the U.S. Census Bureau, the Annie E. Casey Foundation, and FBI Uniform Crime Reports. Where data is estimated or incomplete, we say so.

⚠️ Data note. All figures draw on the Open Baltimore Part 1 Crime Dataset: 499,059 reported incidents, 1 January 2014 – 28 December 2024, 13 crime categories. This captures reported crimes only, neighbourhoods with lower police trust may under-report. The documentary cites a "54% reduction since 2020"; our dataset shows a 40% reduction (332 homicides in 2020 → 199 in 2024), as we do not have data prior to 2014. Observational data cannot prove causation.

The Turning Point

The video opens in the streets of Baltimore before cutting to Mayor Brandon Scott, who makes a single striking claim: for most of his life, the city had over 300 homicides every year. He also points to Redlining, laws that concentrated poverty and disinvestment in specific neighbourhoods, as the deep root of the geographic patterns we see in the crime data today. 2023, he says, is when things finally changed.

Fewer Officers, Fewer Homicides

At 00:04:09, Andrew introduces the Safe Streets program - formerly incarcerated men and women who intervene in conflicts before they turn lethal. They carry no weapons. They carry credibility. The city has fewer police officers than at any point in recent memory, and homicides have fallen to a record low. Whether one caused the other is impossible to say from this data alone. What the city's own Safe Streets programme does document is that since 2015, violence interrupters have mediated over 1,132 conflicts that could have resulted in gun violence, a figure that at least suggests the mechanism is real, even if the causal link remains observational.

Safe Streets, Does It Work?

Safe Streets currently operates in 10 Baltimore neighbourhoods, historically the city's most violence-affected areas. The maps below show where homicides and shootings occurred each year. Zones with a Safe Streets presence are highlighted. Whether the programme caused the decline in these areas is contested, a 2013 peer-reviewed study by Webster et al. (Johns Hopkins) found significant reductions in Cherry Hill and McElderry Park, but mixed results elsewhere. Our own data shows the same neighbourhoods declining, though we cannot rule out city-wide trends as the explanation.

The Kids Left Behind

At 00:09:25, Andrew meets Uncle T, founder of Challenge to Change, a mentorship programme operating out of East Baltimore. The conversation moves to structural conditions: absent fathers, kids out of school, young people with limited economic alternatives. These are correlates of violence, not proven causes, but they are the context in which it happens.

↓ what the data shows

The numbers are moving in the right direction. Disconnected youth has fallen from around 14.5% to roughly 8–9%, a meaningful shift. And the number of children growing up in single-mother households has dropped from 70,000 to 52,000 over the same period. Progress is real.

But look at what remains. Over 52,000 children in Baltimore still grow up without a second parent in the home. Hundreds of teenagers are still caught in gun violence every year. The structural pressures that Uncle T describes , absent fathers, kids with no economic alternative, trauma layered on trauma , have not disappeared. They have eased, slowly, at the margins.

That is precisely why programmes like Challenge to Change still matter. The data does not show a solved problem. It shows a city that has stopped the bleeding, and local figures like Uncle T who are quietly doing the work that no policy, no police reform, and no mayor's office can fully replace. They are already a long way in. They are not done.
